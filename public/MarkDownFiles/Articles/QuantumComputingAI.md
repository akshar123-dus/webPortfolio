# Quantum Computing and Its Impact on Artificial Intelligence

Our world is ever-changing and evolving right before our eyes. Just eighty years ago, the first computer was invented. It could perform some five thousand calculations, hold twenty 10-digit numbers, had no RAM or Hard disk, and weigh thirty tons. However now, the baseline iPhone 16, 48 billion calculations, can hold 68.7 billion 10-digit numbers, has eight GB RAM and 512 GB of storage, and weighs hardly 120 grams. We have come far, quite far. But what is the next big breakthrough, the next leap in technology? Quantum computing.

# What is quantum computing?

Quantum computing is a type of computing that uses the rules of quantum mechanics to process information with quantum bits, or qubits. Unlike regular bits, which are either 0 or 1, qubits can be in many states at once, allowing quantum computers to perform multiple calculations at the same time. This makes them faster at solving certain complex problems. Quantum computers also use special effects like entanglement and interference to improve their power. While still being developed, quantum computing could transform fields like security, problem-solving, and simulations, but challenges like keeping qubits stable and fixing errors are still being worked on.

# History of quantum computing.

Surprisingly the history of quantum computing actually started more than a century ago, in the early 1900s, scientists like Max Planck and Albert Einstein were able to discover and studied quantum mechanics which is basically the behaviours of tiny particles. This laid the foundation for how the quantum computer would later operate. In the 1980s, Richard Feynman and David Deutsch proposed using quantum principles for computing which led to the idea of quantum computers. By the 1990s, quantum computers gained fame and attention when they were able to solve very long and complex algorithms such as Shor’s algorithm. The main achievement was factoring very very large numbers much faster than classical computers. In the 2000s, experimental progress fastened as companies like IBM and D-Wave started developing their very own early quantum computing devices. Then in the 2010s, many significant advancements were observed, like for example Google’s claim of "quantum supremacy" saying that their quantum computer solved a problem which no classical computer could solve in the same amount of time. Today, quantum computing is still in its early stages, but researchers and companies, like IBM, which own the biggest quantum computer in the world, are trying to make it practical for solving challenges in areas like medicine, finance, and logistics.

# Advances in quantum computing and its role in transforming AI applications?

Quantum computing is revolutionizing technology by solving problems far beyond the reach of classical computers. For AI, this means faster training of models, better optimization, and the ability to process complex data more efficiently. For example, Google’s Sycamore quantum processor demonstrated quantum supremacy by solving a computational problem in seconds that would take classical supercomputers thousands of years. Such breakthroughs suggest a future where AI models could train on quantum systems to tackle tasks like drug discovery, climate modeling, or real-time decision-making with unprecedented speed and precision.

# Quantum Computing and AI Security

While quantum computing opens new possibilities, it also introduces security risks, particularly in AI applications. Today’s encryption methods, such as RSA and ECC, are vulnerable to quantum algorithms like Shor's. This could make sensitive AI systems—like those used in healthcare, finance, or national security—susceptible to breaches. To address this, efforts like the National Institute of Standards and Technology (NIST) are working to standardize post-quantum cryptography algorithms, ensuring AI systems remain secure even in a quantum era.

# Precedents in Security:

• The NSA has already begun transitioning to quantum-resistant cryptographic methods, highlighting the urgency of protecting critical systems.
• Companies like Microsoft are incorporating quantum-safe encryption into platforms like Azure Quantum to future-proof their cloud-based AI services.
These examples underscore the dual role of quantum computing in advancing AI capabilities while demanding a proactive approach to security challenges.

# What does the future hold?

Quantum computers aren’t meant to replace the devices we use daily—they’re built for specific problems that classical computers struggle with, like optimizing routes, simulating molecules, or solving complex equations. They’re not better at everything and actually aren’t great for tasks like browsing the web or running apps. Plus, they’re expensive, require super-cold environments, and are tricky to maintain, so it’s unlikely we’ll have one at home. Instead, most people and businesses will access them through the cloud, much like we use online services today. While we’re already seeing some early quantum systems used for research and specialized tasks, widespread commercial deployment for practical applications is likely still 5 to 10 years away as the technology matures and becomes more reliable.
