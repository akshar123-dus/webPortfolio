Tap "buy" on a trading app and your order fills in a blink. Nobody had to hunt for someone who happened to want to sell you that exact stock at that exact moment. So, who was on the other side? More often than not, a market maker. Market makers are firm whose whole job is to always be ready to trade. And some of their most fascinating work happens in derivatives like options and futures. Let's break it down.

## What is a Derivative?

A derivative is a contract whose value is derived from something else, called the underlying: a stock, oil, wheat, a currency, even a whole index. You're not buying the thing itself; you're trading a contract about its price. There are two big types.

## Futures: Locking In Today's Price

Picture a wheat farmer worried prices will crash before harvest, and a bakery worried they'll spike. They agree a price today for wheat delivered in three months. Whatever the market does, both know exactly where they stand. That's a future: an obligation to buy or sell something at a set price on a set date.
•	Traded on exchanges like CME, ICE and Eurex
•	Used by hedgers (an airline locking in fuel costs) and speculators (traders backing a direction)
•	A small deposit called margin controls a much bigger position, so gains and losses are amplified



## Why Options Make It So Much Harder

One stock can have hundreds of options, each with its own strike and expiry, and every price shifts as the stock, time and volatility change. Market makers use models like Black-Scholes (published in 1973, the year the first options exchange opened in Chicago) to estimate fair prices, then hedge to stay neutral: sell someone a call, and you buy some of the stock to offset your risk. That's delta hedging. Traders track the rest of the risk with the Greeks:
•	Delta: how much the option moves when the stock moves £1
•	Gamma: how fast delta itself changes
•	Theta: how much value the option loses each day (time decay)
•	Vega: sensitivity to volatility (fun fact: it's not a real Greek letter)



## Why It Matters

•	Cheaper trading: competing market makers squeeze spreads, lowering costs for everyone from pension funds to students on trading apps
•	Real-world protection: airlines, farmers and businesses use derivatives to guard against swings in fuel, crops and currencies
•	A read on fear: the VIX "fear index" is calculated from S&P 500 option prices
It cuts both ways, though. Leverage can wipe out accounts, complex derivatives played a big part in the 2008 financial crisis, and even pros slip: in 2012, Knight Capital lost over $400 million in under an hour after a faulty software update. That's why risk management is the heart of the job

## Conclusion: The Quiet Engine of Finance

Derivatives and market making rarely make headlines, but they keep modern markets running: cheap trading, protection against risk, and a price on uncertainty. Firms like Optiver, IMC and Jane Street are built around doing it well, blending maths, probability, speed and nerve. So next time you hit "buy" and it fills instantly, remember there's a market maker quietly making it happen.
